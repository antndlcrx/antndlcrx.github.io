## Start here

In the invented example used below, a reader sends an assistant the title of a health article
and asks whether it supports a conclusion they have already reached. One response confidently
agrees and cites a review of twelve trials that does not exist; another asks to see the article's
findings and methods before drawing a conclusion. The first may feel more helpful because it
offers a clear answer, but its reassurance rests on evidence the assistant does not have.
Understanding why a reader might prefer it is a useful part of evaluation, alongside examining
whether the response can support what it claims.

This distinction matters throughout LLM evaluation, from rating an assistant's responses to testing
an agent that runs machine-learning experiments. **A score is evidence for a particular claim under
a particular evaluation setup.** Understanding that setup means knowing what the system was asked
to do, how its work was judged, and which failures the test could reveal.

A useful framework separates human likeability, safety, broad base capabilities, and professional
or complex-task capabilities. These dimensions overlap, but each needs its own evidence. Comparing
preferred answers, evaluating health conversations, and testing research agents shows how the
requirements change as systems take on more demanding work.

## Preference: a useful signal with a limited claim

Pairwise evaluation gives preference a concrete form: a person sees two responses to the same
prompt and chooses one. Combining many such comparisons produces an estimate of relative
preference across the sampled prompts and participating raters. Chatbot Arena uses this approach
to compare assistants, capturing aspects of the experience that a narrowly defined task metric
might miss.

Interpreting the result requires care because several qualities influence a choice. Formatting,
fluency, length, and confidence may all matter, while a reader without the relevant expertise or
source material may struggle to detect an error. Preference can therefore help explain why an
assistant is appealing without establishing how accurately it performs a particular task.

### When the judge is another model

Human judgement is costly to collect at scale, so evaluations also use an LLM-as-a-judge: a model
prompted to rate another model's response. This introduces a second system whose judgements need
validation. The [MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685) documents
limitations including position and verbosity bias, showing why the judge's model, prompt, rubric,
and access to evidence all matter.

For example, judging a literature summary simply by asking how helpful it is combines several
properties that may deserve separate attention. A rubric could assess whether the supplied paper
supports the summary's claims, whether important qualifications survive, whether citations lead
to relevant evidence, and whether the explanation suits its audience. These Oxford LLMs examples
make the evaluation goal specific enough to inspect.

The next step is to check how well the judge applies those criteria. Agreement with expert raters
on representative examples supports its use on similar material. Swapping answer order or changing
the style of an answer while preserving its claims can also reveal sensitivity to features that
should not determine the score. Together, these checks help define where the judge's results are
interpretable and where further scrutiny is needed.

## Risk: the answer can be appealing and still fail

Preference becomes particularly consequential when a system is rewarded for agreement in
situations that call for correction or restraint. Sycophancy describes excessive agreement with
the user's beliefs or preferences at the expense of an accurate or appropriate response.
Research on [sycophancy in language models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548) finds that human
preference feedback can favour agreement over truthfulness. An evaluation concerned with this
failure needs to examine how the model handles mistaken premises, uncertainty, and disagreement.

In vulnerable conversations, these behaviours may have consequences beyond the quality of an
individual answer. A [study of potentially harmful chatbot conversations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16567)
examines a small observational sample and motivates closer scrutiny of possible mental-health
harms. Its findings need to be read within that study design: they do not establish causation or
the prevalence of harm among chatbot users.

Other risks require their own evidence. A fabricated citation, a systematic error affecting one
group, and assistance with dangerous misuse involve different failures, so they need different
test cases and decision rules. Wider societal questions may require a different unit of analysis
altogether. Labour-market exposure estimates, such as those in [GPTs are GPTs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10130),
concern which work tasks might change as capabilities advance; they neither measure an individual
model's correctness nor establish realised job losses.

Even when the requirement is zero critical failures, a finite test can only establish what was
observed under its particular conditions. The number of cases, their coverage, and the uncertainty
around the result remain relevant. Medical evaluation brings these concerns together because both
the nature of an error and its frequency affect how a result should be interpreted.

## HealthBench: expertise becomes a rubric

An exam question can often be scored against a defined answer key, whereas a health conversation
may require several judgements at once. A response can contain correct facts while omitting a
crucial qualification, offering unsupported reassurance, or overlooking the user's circumstances.
HealthBench addresses this problem by evaluating a model's next response in the context of a
conversation, using criteria defined by physicians.

The [HealthBench paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08775) describes 5,000 multi-turn conversations
with 48,562 unique rubric criteria, developed with 262 physicians. Each conversation has its own
weighted rubric for the model's next response. Rather than demanding one canonical wording,
the rubric specifies behaviours that should occur and behaviours that should not.

According to the [official introduction](https://openai.com/index/healthbench/), a GPT-4.1-based
grader checks responses against these criteria. Domain experts define what matters, while the
model grader applies their criteria at scale; physician-authored or validated rubrics therefore
do not imply that physicians independently graded every output. As with other model judges,
understanding the results requires attention to grader validation and inspection of its errors.

### How a conversational rubric changes the measurement

For the title-only article question, a domain rubric would examine whether the response acknowledges
that the evidence is missing, asks for information that could resolve the question, and avoids
inventing support for the reader's conclusion. A response might satisfy some of these requirements
while still introducing an unsupported claim. Separating the criteria makes that combination visible
instead of letting the answer's reassuring tone determine the whole judgement.

HealthBench weights its criteria and includes penalties for undesirable behaviour, so an omission
or harmful statement can affect the result even when the response is otherwise well written.
Those weights are part of the evaluation's design: their relative sizes express how much different
behaviours contribute to the score. The illustration below shows how this works with an invented
rubric and three example responses.

Reading a score alongside the conversation, rubric, and criterion-level judgements makes the
failure easier to diagnose. Instead of a general impression that the model performed badly,
the evaluation can identify an unsupported statement, an omitted action, or an assumption that
the response should have questioned.

### Separate the overall score from specific failures

The same attention to individual criteria helps explain why HealthBench has several variants.
Hard contains 1,000 challenging examples, while Consensus contains 3,671 examples evaluated with
criteria filtered through further physician validation. They address different properties:
the difficulty of the conversation and agreement about what an adequate response should do.
Within these variants, narrower failure slices examine particular kinds of error, as documented
in the [GPT-5 system card, section 3.10](https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-5).

These slices use additional physician validation and report error rates, so lower values indicate
better performance. Their different criteria and denominators also matter: comparing rates across
slices is not equivalent to comparing models on the same test.

The Global Health slice illustrates why context belongs in an evaluation. A medically plausible
response may still assume resources, local risks, or care pathways that the conversation does
not establish. Similar problems arise when social scientists evaluate models across countries
or languages: criteria developed for a dominant setting may overlook what an adequate response
requires elsewhere.

### What HealthBench can establish

Taken together, these results provide evidence about responses to HealthBench's conversation
distribution under its rubric and grading procedure. Because scored text responses represent
only part of a real-world care process, they do not directly measure patient outcomes or certify
a system for clinical deployment.

The approach is useful beyond medicine because it gives domain expertise an explicit role in
scoring. A historian might specify how an answer should handle conflicting primary sources,
while a survey researcher might define which ambiguities a coding system must preserve.
In either case, the evaluation needs to describe the required behaviours and critical failures,
then establish whether its criteria work across the intended population.

## From an answer to a research experiment

Domain criteria become more complicated when a system must carry out work as well as explain it.
A research agent may need to inspect data, write code, run experiments, and produce a result that
someone else can check. Evaluating this workflow requires evidence about its intermediate
decisions and execution, beyond the quality of its final answer.

The distinction is easier to see by comparing tasks. An MMLU-style question tests knowledge
through a constrained answer format, while a [SWE-bench task](https://www.swebench.com/) asks a
system to resolve an issue in a code repository, with executable tests providing part of the
evidence. Completing an ML experiment places further demands on the system: it may choose a
poor plan, struggle with its environment, exhaust its budget, or produce output that cannot be
evaluated. Each benchmark captures a different part of this progression.

### MLE-bench: the model operates inside a scaffold

[MLE-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07095) examines this kind of practical work through 75
Kaggle-derived competitions adapted for local evaluation. An agent works on an ML engineering
problem and produces a submission scored using the competition's task metric, making its sequence
of practical decisions part of the evaluation.

The language model operates within an agent scaffold: software that manages tools, state,
execution, retries, and selection between candidate solutions. The scaffold can shape the search
substantially. Sequential refinement revises a candidate over successive steps, whereas the
branching experiment search used by AIDE explores multiple candidates. For example, separate
branches can pursue different models or training choices rather than continuing to edit the
same solution.

Those opportunities come with resource costs. More branches, retries, or runtime may improve the
chance of finding a strong solution, so a comparison needs to account for how much search each
system can perform. Holding the scaffold, tools, tasks, budget, and scoring constant helps isolate
a model difference. When the aim is to compare complete systems, their designs and constraints
instead become part of what the reported result describes.

### AIRS-Bench: a valid run is only the first hurdle

[AIRS-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06855) extends the comparison to 20 research tasks drawn
from machine-learning papers across several domains. Its
[repository](https://github.com/facebookresearch/airs-bench) provides task definitions and
evaluation code, while the results reproduced here compare models operating within one-shot,
sequential, and tree-search approaches.

An important distinction is whether an agent produces an evaluable submission at all, and how
well that submission performs. AIRS-Bench captures the first with **valid submission rate (VSR)**:
the proportion of valid runs within each task, averaged across tasks. This task-level averaging
matters because pooling all runs can give a different answer when tasks have different numbers
of attempts. **Average normalised score (ANS)** then summarises task performance on a shared
scale, allowing results measured by different task metrics to contribute to an aggregate.

The [scoring convention](https://github.com/facebookresearch/airs-bench#readme) first transforms
distance from the task's theoretical optimum using a negative logarithm, so equal proportional
reductions in the remaining distance produce equal increases in the transformed score. On that transformed
scale, the worst observed run anchors the normalised score at 0 and the cited literature
reference anchors it at 1. Invalid or failed runs receive a normalised score of 0 and remain
in the average, so ANS includes workflow failures as well as the quality of valid results.
The resulting average depends on this transformation and its reference points; it cannot be
interpreted as a mean accuracy or the fraction of tasks completed successfully.

In the historical comparison shown in slides 47–48, the average VSR is 65.8% and the average
ANS is 0.352. Together they describe both the difficulty of reaching an evaluable submission
and the performance achieved under this scoring convention. In particular, 0.352 is neither
a 35.2% task-success rate nor an accuracy of 35.2%. Both figures belong
to the configuration set and run conditions shown in the presentation.

agents exceeded the cited human reference result, even though performance across the full task
set remained modest. The examples below show improvements in coreference resolution, molecular
property prediction, and forecasting, each measured using its own task metric.

All three examples report Gemini 3.1 Pro agents and reproduce historical results from slide 50.
Their metrics need to be read in the appropriate direction: accuracy increases, whereas mean
absolute error decreases. Because relative improvements are computed against each reference
value, they differ from percentage-point changes and should not be averaged across incompatible
raw metrics. The reference results are also tied to the cited papers; establishing whether they
remain state of the art would require a current comparison under equivalent conditions.

The Winogrande result also shows what the agent contributed. Gemini 3.1 Pro acted as the controller
developing the solution, which fine-tuned Qwen2.5-14B using LoRA. The reported accuracy belongs to
that resulting prediction model, rather than to Gemini answering the test questions directly.
The agent's work includes choosing and implementing a way to solve the task; its scaffold and
resource budget affect which solutions it has the opportunity to develop.

These examples show why domain expertise remains necessary even when the result can be scored
automatically. Across medicine, chemistry, social science, and other fields, someone must decide
what constitutes meaningful progress and which interpretations a metric supports. An executable
score makes part of that judgement reproducible without settling the scientific question on its own.

## Why a ranking can change without a model changing

The judge, the rubric, and the agent scaffold each influence the results above. Another part of
the evaluation can change a ranking even when the systems themselves stay fixed: the distribution
of tasks. An aggregate score depends on which cases are included and how much each contributes.
The following illustration isolates that effect by keeping each system's performance within
each type of case constant.

<EvaluationMix />

As the mix changes, so does the overall ranking. A benchmark can therefore suggest a different
model choice from the one suited to your work if the two contain different proportions of cases.
This helps explain the value of HealthBench's urgent and global-health slices: a useful overall
average may conceal performance differences that matter in a particular setting. For research
agents, the relevant breakdown might instead separate classification, forecasting, and molecular
prediction tasks. Reporting these alongside the aggregate makes its weighting easier to assess.

Variation also occurs between attempts at the same task. A long-running agent may fail before
scoring or follow a different sequence of experiments on its next run. Repeated runs help reveal
that variation, provided failed attempts remain in the record. A report of at least one success
in five attempts describes a different level of reliability and resource use from success on a
single attempt, so the selection rule belongs with the result.

## Can we trust the evidence behind the score?

Accounting for the judge, task mix, and run conditions still leaves a question about the test
material itself. As models improve and public benchmarks become widely used, a score may become
less informative for two different reasons: the test may no longer distinguish strong systems,
or exposure to its contents may undermine the claim that it measures generalisation.

The first problem is saturation. When many scores approach a test's ceiling, a harder test can
restore separation, provided its difficulty still represents the capability of interest. A task
that is difficult for reasons unrelated to the intended use would offer little improvement.

The second is contamination, where exposure to test material weakens the evidence of performance
on unseen cases. Establishing exposure can be difficult when the training corpus is unavailable.
Even without known training exposure, public tasks may influence prompt design, scaffold
development, or repeated selection of systems, making the history of benchmark use relevant
to its interpretation.

### Testing for exposure

One approach to investigating exposure is the [black-box contamination study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623),
which compares the likelihood a model assigns to examples in their original dataset order with
the likelihood of shuffled orders. Under the paper's assumptions, a difference can provide
statistical evidence of contamination without access to the full training corpus.

[Recovery of masked benchmark content](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09783) and audits of training
data examine other signals. Each supports a particular kind of exposure claim, while a failure
to detect exposure leaves uncertainty rather than certifying a clean test. It is also necessary
to distinguish learning a skill from seeing its test cases: training on translation examples,
for instance, does not by itself establish exposure to a particular held-out translation benchmark.

### LiveCodeBench: refresh the tasks, retain the conditions

[LiveCodeBench](https://livecodebench.github.io/) addresses part of the exposure problem by
collecting coding problems from periodic contests over time. Dated task windows allow comparisons
on problems released after a relevant cutoff, reducing reliance on a fixed public test set.
The [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974) describes its evaluation across coding scenarios.

New problems still need to be considered alongside the evaluation conditions. A model's training
cutoff may be unclear, tools may retrieve public solutions, and a widely used window may enter
the development process. Release dates, permitted tools, and the specific task window therefore
remain necessary context for reading the leaderboard.

<BenchmarkLive benchmark="livecodebench" />

The exposure question is one instance of a broader requirement: the evidence must match the claim
being made. That requirement becomes particularly demanding when a domain result is used to
support a claim about general intelligence. Different definitions of AGI can require different
evidence, so a numerical threshold needs an argument linking the benchmark task to the proposed definition.

## Bring the evaluation back to your research

Designing an evaluation for your own research brings these decisions together. Domain expertise
determines which behaviours matter, which failures would undermine the work, and which contexts
the test must cover. If an agent will carry out the task, the evaluation also needs a reproducible
environment and a result that can be scored.

Consider an Oxford LLMs example: an agent extracts policy commitments from speeches and returns
supporting passages. As with the title-only health question, the system needs to establish what
the source actually supports before making a claim on its behalf. Before comparing systems, a researcher would need to specify the population
of speeches and languages, define what counts as a commitment, and decide how to adjudicate
ambiguous cases. Requiring a supporting passage makes it possible to distinguish missed
commitments from unsupported extractions or fabricated quotations. Examining errors by group
and document type would then show whether the aggregate conceals failures concentrated in
particular parts of the material.

The same design needs to specify how the system is developed and tested. Keeping development
cases separate from the final test, fixing the model, prompt, tools, scaffold, and budget before
final evaluation, and repeating stochastic runs make the comparison easier to interpret.
Recording these choices allows another researcher to reconstruct what was asked, what counted
as success, and which limitations remain. The reference below collects them in one place.

For the reader who supplied only an article title, a helpful response would explain what remains
unknown and ask for evidence that could resolve it. The rubric makes those requirements explicit
and distinguishes them from the appeal of a confident answer. Research agents extend the same
need for evidence into a workflow whose execution, resources, and variation across attempts also
affect the result. Keeping these observations connected to the intended use gives a favourable
score a meaning that another researcher can examine and defend.
