## Start here

An LLM gives a warm, confident answer to a health question. A reader prefers it to a more cautious
response. Yet it invents supporting evidence, accepts a mistaken premise, or assumes access to
care the reader does not have. The preference is real. **What claim about the system does that
preference actually support?**

The same question applies to human judgements, medical benchmarks, and agents that conduct
machine-learning experiments. As the work becomes more consequential and more autonomous, “Which model is
best?” becomes a question about the task, the evidence, and the conditions under which it was collected.

**An Oxford LLMs teaching companion to Tatiana Shavrina's lecture.** The explanations and added
illustrations are ours; the [original presentation](/materials/lectures/2026/shavrina-llm-agents-and-evaluation.pdf)
is Tatiana's. Reproduced figures retain their historical results, while current source views are
labelled separately. PDF references use page numbers.

The central idea is that **a score is evidence for a particular claim under a particular evaluation
setup**. Evaluation has four distinct axes: human likeability, safety, broad base capabilities,
and professional or complex-task capabilities. Their differences become clear across three
increasingly demanding settings: an answer someone likes, a health conversation that meets
professional standards, and an agent that produces a usable research result.

## Preference: a useful signal with a limited claim

In a pairwise evaluation, a person sees two responses to the same prompt and chooses one.
Many such comparisons estimate relative preference across sampled prompts and participating raters.
Chatbot Arena uses this approach: the result captures what people
choose in that setting, which can be valuable when evaluating an assistant's overall experience.

The limitation appears when we quietly replace “preferred” with “correct” or “reliable.”
Formatting, fluency, length, and confidence can affect a choice. A reader may also lack the
expertise or source material needed to detect an error. Likeability and
task performance need separate evidence, even when they often move together.

### When the judge is another model

An **LLM-as-a-judge** is a model prompted to rate another model's response.
This makes evaluation scalable, but introduces a second system whose judgements need validation.
The [MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685) documents limitations
including position and verbosity bias. A judge's score depends on its model, prompt, rubric,
and the material it can inspect.

For a literature summary, “How helpful is this?” bundles several properties together. A more
informative rubric distinguishes whether claims have support in the supplied paper, whether
qualifications survive the summary, whether citations resolve to relevant evidence, and whether
the explanation suits the intended reader. These are Oxford LLMs examples of turning an
evaluation goal into observable criteria.

Agreement with expert raters on representative examples gives evidence that the judge is useful
for those examples. Swapping answer order or rewriting an answer's style while preserving its
claims can expose sensitivity to irrelevant features. Neither check makes the judge universally
reliable; it helps establish the conditions under which its scores are interpretable.

## Risk: the answer can be appealing and still fail

What happens when a system optimises for being liked in a situation where agreement is the
wrong response? Sycophancy, possible harms in vulnerable conversations, bias, fabricated
information, misinformation, and misuse all belong in evaluation because a pleasant interaction can
conceal a consequential failure.

**Sycophancy** is excessive agreement with the user's beliefs or preferences at the expense of
an accurate or appropriate response. Research on [sycophancy in language models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)
finds that human preference feedback can favour agreement over truthfulness. The relevant
evaluation asks whether the model handles a mistaken premise, uncertainty, or disagreement
well. It does not treat agreement itself as success.

The [study of potentially harmful chatbot conversations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16567) examines a small
observational sample. It motivates scrutiny of possible mental-health harms; it does not establish
causation or the prevalence of harm among chatbot users. That distinction is itself an example
of the central question: what does the evidence support?

Ordinary failures, high-consequence failures, and wider societal risks require different evidence.
A fabricated citation, a systematic error affecting one group, and assistance with dangerous
misuse require different test cases and decision rules. Labour-market exposure estimates,
such as those in [GPTs are GPTs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.10130), ask another question
again: which work tasks might change as capabilities advance? They do not measure an individual
model's correctness or establish realised job losses.

A requirement of zero critical failures expresses a target, not a statistical guarantee.
Observing no failures in a finite test set means none were observed under those conditions.
The denominator, coverage, and uncertainty still matter. Medical evaluation makes this concrete:
it must recognise the kind of error as well as its frequency.

## HealthBench: expertise becomes a rubric

Exam benchmarks test whether a model can select the correct answer to a defined question.
A health conversation is harder to reduce to one answer key. A response may contain several
correct facts while omitting a crucial qualification, giving unsupported reassurance, or failing
to adapt to the user's circumstances. **HealthBench evaluates a model's next response in the
context of a conversation, using criteria defined by physicians.**

The [HealthBench paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.08775) describes 5,000 multi-turn conversations
with 48,562 unique rubric criteria, developed with 262 physicians. Each conversation has its own
weighted rubric for the model's next response. Rather than demanding one canonical wording,
the rubric specifies behaviours that should occur and behaviours that should not.

The [official introduction](https://openai.com/index/healthbench/) describes a GPT-4.1-based
grader that checks a response against those criteria. This separates two roles: domain experts
decide what matters; the grader applies those criteria at scale. Physician-authored or validated
rubrics do not mean every output was independently graded by a physician. Judge validation
and inspection of failures remain part of understanding the measurement.

### How a conversational rubric changes the measurement

Consider the opening example again. A preference comparison may reward warmth and confidence.
A domain rubric can ask additional questions: does the response avoid inventing evidence,
acknowledge missing information, communicate relevant uncertainty, and respond appropriately
to the user's context? An answer can succeed on one criterion and fail on another.

HealthBench attaches weights to criteria, including penalties for undesirable behaviour.
That makes an omission or harmful statement visible in the scoring rather than allowing a
polished overall impression to settle the judgement. The weighting is itself a measurement
choice: it expresses the relative importance of different behaviours and affects the score.

<RubricExplorer />

The useful thing to inspect is therefore more than the headline number. Look at the conversation,
its rubric, the model response, and which criteria the grader marked as met. This turns
“the model did badly” into a diagnosable failure: an unsupported statement, an omitted action,
or an assumption that should have been questioned.

### Separate the overall score from specific failures

HealthBench includes variants designed to examine different aspects of performance. **Hard** contains 1,000 challenging
examples; **Consensus** contains 3,671 examples evaluated with criteria filtered through further
physician validation. Difficulty and agreement about what a response should do are distinct
properties. These benchmark variants should also be distinguished from the narrower failure
slices documented in the [GPT-5 system card, section 3.10](https://deploymentsafety.openai.com/gpt-5).

These slices use additional physician validation. Read the failure-rate
charts with their direction in mind: lower is better for a measured failure, whereas higher is
better for aggregate performance. Results across differently defined slices should not be
compared as if they had the same denominator or difficulty.

Global Health is especially instructive. A response can be medically plausible yet assume
resources, local risks, or care pathways that the conversation does not establish. This is a
failure of contextual fit. For a social scientist evaluating a model across countries or languages,
the same principle applies: a criterion that works for the dominant setting may not describe
success for every population.

### What HealthBench can establish

HealthBench supplies evidence about responses to its conversation distribution under its rubric
and grading procedure. It does not directly measure patient outcomes or certify a system for
clinical deployment. The distinction follows from the task being measured: scored text responses
are one part of a much larger real-world process.

For your own domain, the transferable idea is **expertise expressed as criteria**. A historian can
specify how an answer should handle conflicting primary sources; a survey researcher can define
which ambiguities a coding system must preserve. Useful evaluation makes both the required
behaviours and critical failures explicit, then tests whether the measurement works across the
intended population.

## From an answer to a research experiment

HealthBench asks whether a response meets professional requirements. Research-agent
benchmarks extend the task from responding to **doing**: inspecting data, writing code,
running experiments, and producing a result that can be checked. Knowledge exams, coding,
medicine, and scientific experimentation demand different capabilities; their results cannot
be summarised by one interchangeable leaderboard.

An MMLU-style question tests knowledge through a constrained answer format. A
[SWE-bench task](https://www.swebench.com/) asks a system to resolve a repository issue,
with executable tests providing part of the evidence.
Neither automatically tells us whether an agent can design and complete an ML experiment.
Long workflows introduce additional points of failure: a mistaken plan, broken environment,
wasted budget, invalid output, or misleading interpretation of a result.

### MLE-bench: the model operates inside a scaffold

[MLE-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.07095) contains 75 Kaggle-derived
competitions adapted for local evaluation. Agents work on ML engineering problems and their
submissions are evaluated against the competition's task metric. This tests a sequence of
practical decisions, rather than an explanation of how an experiment ought to work.

The **agent scaffold** is the software that turns a language model into that workflow: it manages
tools, state, execution, retries, and selection between candidate solutions. Sequential refinement
revises a candidate over successive steps; the branching experiment search used by AIDE explores
multiple candidates. A branch can pursue
a different model or training choice, allowing exploration beyond repeatedly editing one candidate.

Search is also an allocation of resources. More branches, retries, or runtime can improve the
chance of finding a strong solution, but change the comparison. To isolate a model difference,
hold the scaffold, tools, tasks, budget, and scoring constant. To compare complete systems,
describe each design and the constraints that make the comparison meaningful.

### AIRS-Bench: a valid run is only the first hurdle

[AIRS-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.06855) uses 20 research tasks
drawn from machine-learning papers across several domains. The
[repository](https://github.com/facebookresearch/airs-bench) exposes task definitions and
evaluation code. The lecture's experiments compare models within one-shot, sequential, and
tree-search approaches.

Two measurements tell complementary stories. **Valid submission rate (VSR)** records whether
a run produces a submission that can be evaluated. Valid-run proportions are computed within
each task and then averaged across tasks; this need not equal the proportion obtained by pooling
all runs when run counts differ. **Average normalised score (ANS)** measures
task performance across different task metrics. VSR asks whether the workflow reached the
scoring stage; ANS asks what it achieved under the benchmark's scoring convention.

The [scoring convention](https://github.com/facebookresearch/airs-bench#readme) first transforms
distance from the task's theoretical optimum using a negative logarithm. On that transformed
scale, the worst observed run anchors the normalised score at 0 and the cited literature
reference anchors it at 1. Invalid or failed runs receive a normalised score of 0 and remain
in the average, so ANS includes workflow failures as well as the quality of valid results.
These reference points matter: ANS is not simply a mean of accuracies, nor the fraction of
tasks completed successfully.

The lecture reports VSR of **65.8%** and ANS of **0.352** on average in the displayed evaluation.
The latter does not mean “35.2% of tasks solved” or “35.2% of scientific ability.” The first
number describes reaching a valid submission; the second is a benchmark-defined summary
of performance. Both refer to the lecture's configuration set and run conditions.

On selected tasks, evaluated agents exceeded the cited human reference result. That is compatible
with modest average performance. Success on several
tasks and reliability across the full task set are different claims. Also distinguish the paper's
reference result from a claim about the best result available today: a “SOTA” label needs a date,
a task definition, and a comparable evaluation protocol.

Across medicine, chemistry, social science, and other fields, a benchmark must encode what
meaningful progress looks like in that domain. This makes domain specialists central to its

## Why a ranking can change without a model changing

A score is a property of the model **and** its evaluation system. Several parts of that system
shape the result: the judge in preference evaluation,
the rubric in HealthBench, and the scaffold and budget in research-agent benchmarks. One more
is the **task distribution**: which cases count, and how often.

<EvaluationMix />

In this Oxford LLMs illustration, changing the mix of cases changes the aggregate score.
A benchmark with a different case mix
from your actual work can suggest a different model choice even if performance within each
type of case is unchanged.

HealthBench's failure slices make this concrete: overall performance and performance on urgent
or global-health cases answer different questions. For research agents, the mix might instead
be classification, forecasting, or molecular prediction tasks. Report the overall result alongside
breakdowns that matter to the intended use, and explain how the aggregate weights them.

Long workflows also require repeated runs. An execution can fail before scoring, or explore a
different sequence of experiments. Report variation and retain failed attempts. “At least one
success in five attempts” and “success on a single attempt” have different denominators and
resource costs; neither should silently stand in for the other.

## Can we trust the evidence behind the score?

Even a carefully chosen metric can become weak evidence if the test no longer measures what
we think it does. Saturation and contamination can both weaken a benchmark's usefulness, but
they call for different checks. Continuously refreshed tests address some of these concerns.

**Saturation** occurs when a test stops separating strong systems because many scores approach
its ceiling. A harder test can restore separation, provided the new difficulty still represents
the capability of interest. Difficulty alone does not make a benchmark relevant.

**Contamination** occurs when exposure to test material weakens the claim that the result
demonstrates generalisation to unseen cases. Exposure may be difficult to establish when a
training corpus is unavailable. Public tasks also create opportunities for adaptation through
prompting, scaffold development, or repeated selection, even when training exposure is unknown.

### Testing for exposure

The [black-box contamination study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17623) exploits
an unusual feature: a model may assign greater likelihood to examples in their original dataset
order than to shuffled orders. Under the paper's assumptions, that difference can provide
statistical evidence of contamination without inspection of the full training corpus.

Other approaches include [recovery of masked benchmark content](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09783)
and audits of training data. These techniques test different signals. Detection supports a
specific exposure claim; failure to detect does not certify a clean test. Similarly, exposure to
examples of a skill, such as translation, is not automatically exposure to a particular held-out
benchmark. Keep the training question and the test-leakage question distinct.

### LiveCodeBench: refresh the tasks, retain the conditions

[LiveCodeBench](https://livecodebench.github.io/) collects coding
problems from periodic contests over time. A dated task window helps compare models on problems
released after a relevant cutoff, rather than relying indefinitely on the same public test set.
The [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07974) explains its evaluation across coding scenarios.

Freshness helps, but it is not a certificate of non-exposure. A model's training cutoff may be
unclear; tools may retrieve public solutions; a widely used window may become part of development.
Check the release dates, allowed tools, evaluation setting, and the specific window behind a score.
A current leaderboard is most useful when these conditions travel with the ranking.

The same discipline applies to broad claims about intelligence. Definitions of AGI can differ.
A result on a domain benchmark supports a domain claim under its test conditions;
it cannot resolve a broader definition merely by crossing a numerical threshold.

## Bring the evaluation back to your research

Creating a benchmark begins with domain expertise: someone must decide which behaviours are required, which
failures are consequential, and which contexts the test must cover. Research-agent benchmarks
add the need to define a reproducible environment and a result that can actually be scored.

For a concrete Oxford LLMs example, suppose an agent extracts policy commitments from speeches
and returns supporting passages. A defensible claim would identify the speech population and
languages, define what counts as a commitment, and require evidence for each extraction. The
evaluation would separately measure missed commitments, unsupported extractions, and fabricated
quotations, then inspect which groups or document types bear the errors.

Freeze the model, prompt, tools, scaffold, and budget before final evaluation. Keep development
cases separate from the final test; define how ambiguous cases are adjudicated; repeat runs when
the workflow is stochastic. The result becomes useful because a reader can reconstruct what
was asked, what counted as success, and where the evidence stops.

The opening health answer is now easier to evaluate. Preference tells us how it was received;
a domain rubric tells us which requirements it met; targeted failure slices reveal errors an
average might hide. Extending the system into an agent adds execution, resources, and repeated
attempts to the same argument. The question remains: **what claim does this evidence support?**
